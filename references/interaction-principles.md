# Interaction principles

Seven principles from Don Norman's work, as the Laws of UX cards by Jon Yablonski (lawsofux.com, 2022) frame them. Paraphrased, not copied.

## Discoverability

The ability to discover what a system does, how it works, and what operations are possible.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Discoverability is how people learn what is possible. It measures how easily users find new content or features, it shapes whether they can finish a task, and it emerges when affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings and feedback are applied well.
- Give the screen clear focal points. Strong visual hierarchy, visible critical elements, navigation that is easy to reach and read, and icons with widely understood meaning all help people discover what the product offers.
- Keep the distinction between discoverability and findability in mind. Findability covers reaching something users already expect to exist. Discoverability covers stumbling on content or functions they did not know were there.

Origin: Don Norman, researcher, professor and author, defined this as one of his fundamental principles of interaction, applied to make products efficient, effective and pleasant to use. It grows out of five underlying psychological concepts: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings and feedback.
Related: Conceptual Model, Usability Test.

## Affordances

Define what actions are possible with an object or interface based on the capabilities of the user.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Affordances tell people which action to take. They help users reach a goal by matching what an object does to the conceptual model users already hold of what it should do.
- The form of an interactive element should imply its function, so people work out possible actions with no labels or instructions. A button reads as pressable because it looks raised, or because it is styled apart from surrounding content, often with action wording on top.
- Keep the distinction between signifier and affordance clear. Affordances are the actions that can happen. Signifiers show where those actions happen.

Origin: Don Norman, researcher, professor and author, defined this as one of his fundamental principles of interaction, applied to make products efficient, effective and pleasant to use. It grows out of five underlying psychological concepts: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings and feedback.

## Signifiers

Signifiers are visible or audible clues that communicate the appropriate action within a system.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Signifiers provide the important cues. They show how and where people can act on an interface and make an object's affordances visible.
- Apply signifiers to communicate the "what". Labels, arrows, icons and sounds steer users toward the right action. On a door, a flat horizontal bar says push and a handle says pull.
- Keep the distinction between signifier and affordance clear. Affordances are the actions that can happen. Signifiers show where those actions happen.

Origin: Don Norman, researcher, professor and author, defined this as one of his fundamental principles of interaction, applied to make products efficient, effective and pleasant to use. It grows out of five underlying psychological concepts: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings and feedback.

## Feedback

System responses that makes it clear to the user what action has been taken and what has been accomplished.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Feedback communicates the current state of a system or object. It lets users feel in control, choose the right next step toward their goal, and trust that the system works as intended.
- Good design gives clear feedback, which heads off discomfort and difficulty. A loading bar tells people that progress is happening out of sight, and with no bar they may assume nothing is happening. When something completes very fast, a brief progress indicator can reassure people that the action went through.
- Avoid ambiguity and stay clear and specific. Design so the user never has to guess which action they took or what came of it.

Origin: Don Norman, researcher, professor and author, defined this as one of his fundamental principles of interaction, applied to make products efficient, effective and pleasant to use. It grows out of five underlying psychological concepts: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings and feedback.
Related: Mapping, Usability Test.

## Mapping

The relationship between the elements of two sets of things.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Mapping helps people understand what controls will do. When controls match the layout of the things they control, working out how to use them gets easier. Turning a steering wheel clockwise sends the car right, and a bank of light switches laid out to match the ceiling lights is easy to read.
- Natural mapping uses spatial analogies, such as dragging an element up or down to raise or lower another value, and these cut the time it takes to understand a control.

Origin: Don Norman, researcher, professor and author, borrowed the term mapping from mathematics to describe the relationship between elements of two sets. He counts it among the six concepts needed to support discoverability and understanding.
Related: Conceptual Model, Usability Test.

## Constraints

Physical, semantic, cultural and logical constraints guide our actions and aid in interpretation.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Constraints give us clues. By narrowing the set of possible actions they help people settle on a course of action.
- Physical constraints restrict which operations are possible. A mouse cursor stops at the screen edge, and that boundary keeps users from losing their visual anchor.
- Semantic constraints hint at where an action can happen. A socket only accepts plug pins one way, and that single orientation removes a class of user error.
- Cultural constraints come from social convention. Queueing to be served tells people where to stand without any sign.
- Logical constraints narrow the alternatives. Scroll bars signal that content runs past the viewport and that scrolling is the move.

Origin: Constraints belong to the fundamental principles of interaction defined by American researcher, professor and author Don Norman, applied to make products and services efficient, effective and pleasant to use.
Related: Cognitive Load, Usability Test.

## Conceptual Model

An explanation, usually highly simplified, of how something works, which is formed through experience, training and instruction.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Conceptual models provide understanding. They let people connect something new to things they already know, so they grasp how it works and what to do when it goes wrong.
- Lean on universal ideas everyone shares. Files, folders and app icons on computers are widely recognised, which makes them easy to understand and use. Abstract actions such as deleting borrow a visual metaphor, the trash icon, because everyone understands throwing something away.
- Mismatched conceptual models cause frustration. Without a sound model people act hastily, fail to appreciate why a system behaves as it does, and get annoyed when it defies their expectations.

Origin: The conceptual model belongs to the fundamental principles of interaction defined by American researcher, professor and author Don Norman, applied to make products and services efficient, effective and pleasant to use.
Related: Discoverability, User Interview.

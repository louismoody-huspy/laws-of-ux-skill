# UX theory, part one

Fourteen laws and effects, as the Laws of UX cards by Jon Yablonski (lawsofux.com, 2022) frame them. Paraphrased, not copied.

## Aesthetic-usability effect

Users often perceive aesthetically pleasing design as design that's more usable.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Good aesthetics trigger a positive reaction, so people judge an attractive interface to work better than an equally usable plain one.
- Tolerance for small usability flaws goes up when the product looks good.
- Attractive visuals can hide usability problems, so issues may slip past usability testing.

Origin: Hitachi Design Center researchers Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura ran the first HCI study of this in 1995, asking 252 people to rate 26 ATM interface variants for ease of use and visual appeal. Perceived ease of use tracked visual appeal far more closely than it tracked measured ease of use, so people judge an interface by its looks even while trying to judge its function.
Related: Doherty threshold, Selective attention.

## Doherty threshold

Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures that neither has to wait on the other.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- System feedback should land within 400 ms so attention holds and people keep working.
- Perceived performance is a lever: make the response feel faster and the wait feels shorter.
- Animation gives people something to watch while loading or processing runs behind the scenes.
- Progress bars make a wait bearable even when their accuracy is rough.
- A purposeful delay can raise the perceived value of a process and build trust, even when the real work finishes much sooner.

Origin: Walter J. Doherty and Ahrvind J. Thadani published a paper in 1982 that reset the required computer response time from the old 2,000 ms standard to 400 ms. A command answered in under 400 ms was said to clear the Doherty threshold.
Related: Aesthetic-usability effect, Flow.

## Fitts' law

The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Size matters: touch targets need to be big enough for people to hit them accurately.
- Spacing matters: leave generous room between touch targets.
- Placement matters: put touch targets where they are easy to reach.

Origin: Psychologist Paul Fitts studied the human motor system in 1954 and showed that movement time grows with distance to a target and shrinks as the target gets bigger, with fast moves to small targets producing more errors. The law shaped the convention of large buttons, especially on finger-driven mobile screens, and of keeping task controls close to where the user is already working.
Related: Hick's law, Cognitive load.

## Goal-gradient effect

The tendency to approach a goal increases with proximity to the goal.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Proximity is an accelerant: the nearer people get to finishing a task, the faster they move.
- Motivation can be manufactured, since a head start of artificial progress keeps people committed to finishing.
- Progress shown clearly pushes people to complete the task.

Origin: Behaviourist Clark Hull proposed the goal-gradient hypothesis in 1932 and in 1934 showed rats speeding up along a straight alley as they neared the food. It has been studied widely in animals and less so in people, where it matters for reward programmes and other motivational systems.
Related: Zeigarnik effect, Selective attention.

## Hick's law

The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Minimise choices where response time matters, so decisions come faster.
- Smaller steps cut cognitive load, so split complex tasks up.
- Recommendations keep people from feeling overwhelmed, so highlight the suggested option.
- Progressive onboarding keeps the load light for new users.
- Simplification has a limit: strip too much away and the interface turns abstract.

Origin: Named for British psychologist William Edmund Hick and American psychologist Ray Hyman, who in 1952 examined how the number of stimuli affects reaction time to any one of them. More options means more time spent interpreting and deciding, which is work people did not ask for.
Related: Fitts' law, Cognitive load.

## Jakob's law

Users spend most of their time on other sites. This means that users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Expectations travel: people carry what they learned on one familiar product over to anything that looks similar.
- Existing mental models are an asset, since building on them lets people focus on their task instead of learning a new system.
- Discord from change is reduced by letting people keep the familiar version for a limited time.

Origin: Coined by Jakob Nielsen, user advocate and principal of the Nielsen Norman Group, which he co-founded with Dr Donald Norman, formerly VP of Research at Apple. Nielsen started the discount usability engineering movement and invented several usability methods, among them heuristic evaluation.
Related: Mental model, Occam's razor.

## Law of common region

Elements tend to be perceived into groups if they are sharing an area with a clearly defined boundary.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Structure and relationship become clear when a common region shows how elements and sections belong together.
- Borders around an element or group are the easiest way to create a common region.
- Backgrounds behind an element or group create a common region too.

Origin: Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Kohler set out principles in the early twentieth century describing how people perceive objects as organised patterns. The Gestalt principles fall into five groups: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure and connectedness.
Related: Law of uniform connectedness, Selective attention.

## Law of proximity

Objects that are near to, or proximate to each other, tend to be grouped together.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Relationship is implied by nearness: things placed close together read as related.
- Perception follows distance, so elements sitting close are assumed to share a function or trait.
- Organising information with proximity helps people understand it faster and with less effort.

Origin: Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Kohler set out principles in the early twentieth century describing how people perceive objects as organised patterns. The Gestalt principles fall into five groups: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure and connectedness.
Related: Law of similarity, Selective attention.

## Law of Prägnanz

People will perceive and interpret ambiguous or complex images as the simplest form possible, because it is the interpretation that requires the least cognitive effort.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Simplicity and order are what the eye hunts for in complex shapes, because that keeps information from overwhelming us.
- Visual processing favours simple figures: research shows people process and remember them better than complex ones.
- Unified shape is the eye's shortcut, as it collapses complex forms into one simple whole.

Origin: Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Kohler set out principles in the early twentieth century describing how people perceive objects as organised patterns. The Gestalt principles fall into five groups: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure and connectedness.
Related: Tesler's law, Selective attention.

## Law of similarity

The human eye tends to perceive similar elements in a design as a complete picture, shape or group, even if those elements are separated.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Visual similarity signals relationship: elements that look alike read as related.
- Common meaning is implied by shared colour, shape, size, orientation or movement, so matching elements are assumed to belong together and behave the same way.
- Links and navigation need to look different from ordinary text so people can tell them apart.

Origin: Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Kohler set out principles in the early twentieth century describing how people perceive objects as organised patterns. The Gestalt principles fall into five groups: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure and connectedness.
Related: Law of proximity, Selective attention.

## Law of uniform connectedness

Elements that are visually connected are perceived as more related than elements with no connection.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Grouping related functions with shared colours, lines, frames or shapes makes their connection visible.
- Connection can also be drawn directly, with a line or arrow running from one element to the next.
- Context and relationship between similar items can be emphasised with uniform connectedness.

Origin: Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Kohler set out principles in the early twentieth century describing how people perceive objects as organised patterns. The Gestalt principles fall into five groups: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure and connectedness.
Related: Law of common region, Selective attention.

## Miller's law

The average person can only keep seven (plus or minus two) items in their working memory.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- The magical number seven is no excuse for needless design limits.
- Chunks make content easier to process, understand and remember, so break it up.
- Short-term memory capacity differs from person to person, shaped by prior knowledge and the situation.

Origin: George Miller claimed in 1956 that immediate memory span and absolute judgement both top out at around seven pieces of information. He measured information in bits, with each bit a choice between two equally likely options, and called the point where confusion produces wrong judgements the channel capacity, the amount of information a channel carries reliably in a given time.
Related: Hick's law, Chunking.

## Occam's razor

Among competing hypotheses that predict equally well, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Reducing complexity works best by never adding it in the first place.
- Analyse every element and remove as many as you can while the whole still does its job.
- Completion arrives only once nothing else can be taken away.

Origin: Occam's razor, also Ockham's razor or the law of parsimony, is a problem-solving principle that says to pick the candidate answer that makes the fewest assumptions. It is attributed to William of Ockham, an English Franciscan friar, scholastic philosopher and theologian who lived from around 1287 to 1347.
Related: Tesler's law, Analysis paralysis.

## Pareto principle

For many events, roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Distribution of inputs and outputs is rarely even.
- Contributors that matter may be a small handful inside a large group.
- Effort belongs on the areas that bring the biggest benefit to the most users.

Origin: Economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed that 20% of Italy's population owned 80% of its land. The 80/20 lens may sound loose, yet it gives a sharp, widely applicable way to analyse lopsided systems, including UX strategy.
Related: Parkinson's law, Analysis paralysis.

# Psychology concepts

Ten concepts about how people perceive and think, as the Laws of UX cards by Jon Yablonski (lawsofux.com, 2022) frame them. Paraphrased, not copied.

## Contextual Inquiry

A field study that involves in-depth observation and interviews of a small sample of users to gain a robust understanding of work practices and behaviors.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Open with an introduction: say who you are, what the inquiry is for, what the session will look like, and that everything they share stays confidential.
- Inform the participant before the interview starts that you will watch them do their normal work and will interrupt with questions whenever something catches your eye.
- Explore while they work: observe, pause them to dig into moments you want to understand, and ask open questions that let them explain why they did what they did.
- Clarify at the end by raising any leftover questions and playing back your reading of what you saw, so they can correct it.
- Synthesize afterwards by pulling the data from all inquiries into patterns and themes, affinity mapping being one way to do that.

Related: User Interview.

## Cognitive Load

The amount of mental resources needed to understand and interact with an interface.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Our brains have limited processing power, so once incoming information outgrows that capacity tasks get harder, details slip and people feel swamped.
- Intrinsic cognitive load is the effort of holding goal-relevant information, taking in new information and tracking the goal itself, and working memory carries only three or four items at a time.
- Extraneous cognitive load is processing that burns resources without helping anyone understand the content, such as decorative or needless design elements.

Origin: John Sweller built cognitive load theory in the late 1980s from a problem-solving study, extending George Miller's information processing work. He argued instructional design can lower the load on learners, and set this out in a 1988 paper on cognitive load theory, learning difficulty and instructional design.
Related: Hick's Law, Affordances.

## Cognitive Bias

Systematic errors of thinking or rationality in judgement that influence our perception of the world and our decision-making ability.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Cognitive biases make us efficient: instead of reasoning through every situation we build rules of thumb from past experience, and these shortcuts let us decide fast while also steering judgement without us noticing.
- Confirmation bias is the example to remember, the pull to seek, read and recall information that backs what we already believe, which is why a reasoned discussion on a divisive topic gets so hard.
- Building awareness of our own biases will not remove them, yet it helps us spot them and guards against faulty reasoning, unintended discrimination and expensive decisions.

Origin: Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman introduced cognitive biases in 1972 after noticing how poorly people reason intuitively about large orders of magnitude. Their repeatable experiments with colleagues showed human judgement and decision making sit apart from rational choice theory.
Related: Peak-End Rule, Conceptual Model.

## Cognitive Dissonance

When a user is confronted with an interface or affordance that appears to be intuitive but delivers unexpected results.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Cognitive dissonance damages the experience: it frustrates people and can push them to drop the task or leave altogether.
- Seek out the frustrating and confusing steps early by interviewing users to learn their mental model, drawing task flows so each step follows coherently, and shaping the information architecture with card sorting.
- Evaluate and test for a smooth journey, since expert reviews and usability tests surface problems and point to fixes; an experience feels easy because no step along the way was confusing or hard.

Origin: Leon Festinger established the idea in two works, a 1956 study of a group that predicted the end of the world and his 1957 theory of cognitive dissonance. He proposed that people need internal psychological consistency to function, and that inconsistency makes them uncomfortable and drives them to resolve it.
Related: Mental Model, Jakob's Law.

## Mental Model

An explanation of someone's thought process about how something works in the real world.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Match designs to the mental models users already hold, so they carry knowledge from one product to the next without first having to learn how the new system works.
- Meet users' expectations and subvert them at your peril: ecommerce sites keep product cards, carts and checkout flows consistent for this reason, and redesigning or removing those concepts for novelty risks losing people.
- Understand how your users think, because closing the gap between our mental models and theirs is one of the hardest parts of UX design, and interviews, personas, journey maps and empathy maps are the tools for it.

Origin: The term is thought to come from Kenneth Craik's 1943 book The Nature of Explanation. It has since been discussed and applied widely in human computer interaction and usability.
Related: Jakob's Law, Conceptual Model.

## Chunking

Chunking is a process by which individual pieces of an information set are broken down and then grouped together in a meaningful whole.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Chunking lets people scan quickly, pick out what matches their goal and process it, so they finish the task sooner.
- Grouping content into visually distinct sets with clear hierarchy lines the information up with how people evaluate and digest content.
- Content relationships become clearer when you chunk: group content into distinct modules, separate it with rules and give it hierarchy so the underlying connections show.

Origin: The word comes from George A. Miller's 1956 paper on the magical number seven, plus or minus two. As information theory entered psychology, Miller saw that some cognitive tasks behaved like a channel with a fixed capacity in bits, while short-term memory did not.
Related: Miller's Law, Mapping.

## Selective Attention

The process of focusing our attention only to a subset of the stimuli in the environment, usually those related to our goals.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- People filter out what is irrelevant, so guide their attention: keep them on task by helping them find the information or action they need at the right moment, rather than overwhelming or distracting them.
- Banner blindness means visitors skip anything that looks like an ad or sits where ads usually sit, so content styled or placed like an ad will be ignored, and content that avoids this gets seen.
- Change blindness happens when a significant change in the interface goes unnoticed because attention is limited and the cues are weak, so check the design for changes that happen at the same time and pull attention away from each other.

Origin: Donald Broadbent's filter model was an early theory of attention, built on Cherry Collins's research. He described attention as information processing, with limited capacity and the selection of what to process happening early in perception.
Related: Doherty Threshold, Signifiers.

## Analysis Paralysis

The inability to make a decision due to over-thinking a problem.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Too many options hurt people's ability to decide, and that can colour how they feel about the whole experience.
- Optimise for choice by keeping the decision process in mind: show one thing at a time, such as a featured product, or offer search and filtering up front so people narrow the field themselves.
- Optimise for comparison when a comparison is unavoidable, by placing related items and options side by side, pricing tiers being the classic case.

Origin: The idea runs through stories as far back as the fable of the fox and the cat in Aesop's Fables, and the two words were first paired in an 1803 pronouncing dictionary because they sound alike. In 1956 Charles R. Schwartz used the phrase in a management article on return on investment as a decision tool.
Related: Hick's Law, Affordances.

## Flow

The mental state in which a person performing an activity is fully immersed in a feeling of energized focus, full involvement and enjoyment of process of the activity.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Flow comes from immersive, engaging experiences: it arises when task difficulty matches the skill needed, and it feels like intense concentration on the present with a sense of total control.
- Designing for flow means giving feedback so people know what they did and what got done, and optimising task efficiency through a responsive system, less friction and content that can be discovered intuitively, so they stay engaged.
- Finding balance matters: a task that is too hard frustrates, one that is too easy bores, so match the challenge to the user's skill.

Origin: Psychologist Mihály Csíkszentmihályi coined flow in 1975 and it has been picked up across many fields, occupational therapy among them. Some argue the idea existed for thousands of years under other names.
Related: Doherty Threshold, Discoverability.

## Short-Term Memory

The capacity to store a small amount of information in mind and keep it readily available for a short period of time.

Use it when designing or reviewing:
- Short-term memory is limited in capacity and duration: we hold around seven chunks at once and each fades within 20 to 30 seconds, we use it to track information while completing tasks, and we struggle to remember what we already saw, so show only what is necessary and relevant.
- Prioritise recognition over recall, because we recognise things we have seen far better than we hold new information ready, so mark what has already been viewed with visited link styling and breadcrumbs.
- Place the burden of memory on the system rather than the user by carrying critical information from screen to screen, as a comparison table does when weighing several items.

Origin: Memory has been split into short-term and long-term stores since the 19th century. The classical model from the 1960s, known as the modal model, holds that memories move from short-term into long-term storage over time, and Atkinson and Shiffrin set it out most fully in 1968.
Related: Affordances.

# UX methods

Eight research and definition methods, as the Laws of UX cards by Jon Yablonski (lawsofux.com, 2022) frame them. Paraphrased, not copied.

## Card sorting

A research technique in which users organize topics into groups to create an information architecture that suits their expectations.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- Gather the topics first: pick the items that stand for the core content of your structure, such as navigation entries or catalogue products, and put each one on its own card.
- Sort by asking participants to place the cards one by one into groups that feel right to them, and prompt them to talk through their thinking as they go.
- Label each group by asking the participant to name it in their own words, which exposes their mental model and feeds the category names you choose later.
- Dig deeper by asking why each group exists, where they struggled, and what they make of any cards left over.

Related: Hick's Law, Contextual Inquiry.

## Design principles

An agreed-upon set of guidelines that help frame how a design team approaches and solves problems.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- Rally the team together early, because the wider the group that shapes the principles, the more readily the whole team adopts them.
- Define the criteria of your principles on sticky notes before writing any, for instance that they must be specific, user led and scalable, and decide whether they cover service, interaction or content design, or all three.
- Diverge for ten minutes with everyone writing as many candidate principles as they can.
- Converge by sharing ideas in turn, clustering them on the wall into themes, then dot voting with three votes each on the themes that resonate most.
- Refine and apply by testing the principles against real or imagined work, merging overlaps, sharpening the wording until it is clear and specific, and planning how to keep them in daily use.

Related: Affinity Mapping.

## Journey mapping

A visualization of the process that a user goes through in order to accomplish a goal.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- The lens row sets up who the user is, what they are trying to do, and what they expect, for instance a rider ordering a car who expects pickup at the exact spot within ten minutes.
- The experience row plots the user's actions and feelings across phases on a timeline, with a curve on the vertical axis showing where delight rises and frustration dips.
- The insights row turns pain points into opportunities, names the internal team that owns each one, and attaches a metric to track it, such as live driver location owned by product and measured by post ride ratings.

Related: Peak-End Rule, Affinity Mapping.

## User personas

A fictional representation of users whose characteristics and goals represent that of a larger group of users.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- The info section makes the persona memorable for the team with a photo, tagline, name, age and occupation, drawn from what the members of that audience segment have in common.
- The details section outlines behaviours, motivations, goals and tasks, with a short bio for narrative, frustrations and sources of joy, and the tasks the persona performs in the product, all chosen to build empathy around what affects the design.
- The insights section captures attitude and mindset through direct quotes from research and any extra context that sharpens the persona.

Related: Jakob's Law, Contextual Inquiry.

## Usability test

An observational method to uncover problems in a design, discover opportunities that exist, and learn more about the behaviors and preferences of users.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- Recruit real users who represent the audience, and treat feedback from colleagues as a stand in only when the product is built solely for them.
- Simulate real tasks by giving participants realistic things to do with the design, phrased as tightly or as openly as the test goals require.
- Observe and listen with neutrality, skip leading questions, make clear the design is under test and the person is helping, and resist rescuing them when they get stuck, since watching them recover is where the insight lives.
- Set and measure performance metrics for speed and ease of completion alongside what participants say, because how well people do and how they feel about it often differ.

Related: Jakob's Law, Affinity Mapping.

## User interview

A UX research technique during which a researcher asks one user questions about a particular topic in order to gain insights.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- Define the goal of the interview as one concise question about a specific aspect of user behaviour or attitude, so every answer feeds the design.
- Prepare your discussion guide with questions built around that goal, leave room for follow ups, and a loose set of themes often works well as the guide, since free conversation surfaces unexpected findings.
- Build rapport with the interviewee by asking whether they have done this before and reassuring them it is a relaxed chat, because people share more once they trust the interviewer.
- Avoid leading questions, yes or no questions, and vague ones; open with what, how, when, or tell me about, to draw out rich and unbiased answers.

Related: Jakob's Law, Affinity Mapping.

## Affinity mapping

A method for categorizing and sorting qualitative data or observations using an affinity diagram in order to identify themes and gain insights.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- Record every finding from interviews, tests or other research on its own sticky note, covering context, observations, quotes, common slips and use cases.
- Identify patterns by grouping related notes without overthinking it, since the aim is to see the data as a whole and groups can shift later.
- Label each group with a theme stated as a sentence, in the shape of people often did one thing when trying to achieve another.
- Insights come from asking what story the themes tell together, then summarising each with supporting evidence and a design action attached.

Related: User Interview.

## UX survey

A qualitative method of collecting data about a user's interactions and experience with a website or digital product.

Use it when planning research or testing:
- Set expectations up front by telling participants what you will ask, how long it takes, and whom to contact if something goes wrong.
- Keep questions short and simple to lift completion and answer quality, mix fixed choice prompts with free text ones, and ask one thing at a time.
- Bias creeps in through questions that lead or prime, so ask people to describe their experience with a feature, and drop any phrasing that presumes they had problems.
- Ease in with broad, easy questions before moving to those that demand more thought or effort.

Related: Usability Test, Contextual Inquiry.
